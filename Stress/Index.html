Syllabus

Week 1 (Sept 24)

  • Syllabus
  • Overview handout on stress and feet.
  • Selkirk (1980) makes the case for a foot-based theory of English stress.
  • McCarthy (1982) expands the case for feet beyond stress.
  • Hayes (1982) argues for a different foot-based theory that complicates foot structure while simplifying the stress-footing interface.

Week 2 (Oct 1)

  • Martinez-Paricio & Kager (2021) argue for recursive feet of the type from the Selkirk proposal.
  • Golston (2021) responds with a counterargument against such feet.
  • The papers above are overviews of theoretical positions. Bennett (2013), in constrast, develops a detailed analysis of stress and epenthesis in Huariapano using recursive feet.
  • Lunden (2017) notes acoustic asymmetries between immediately post-tonic and 'lapse-final' vowels in English nonce-word production, relevant to minimal and maximal feet. Jonah will share additional data from the Buckeye corpus and maybe a recent production exeriment on consonants and vowels across the two types of position. No need to read this one in too much detail, it's just a simple set of results we're interested in.

Week 3 (Oct 8)
We'll be discussing Dynamic Attending Theory, which models broadly 'metrical' or rhythmic perception as a process of coupling two oscillations, one from external events and one from attention. There's a lot here, so here's our recommendations:

  • Beier & Ferreira (2018) give a nice overview of how this approach has been brought to bear on speech. If you read one thing, read this; Khang will lead discussion.
  • Large & Jones (1999) is the full, heavy-duty theoretical and mathematical background, with mostly musical examples. If you're into dynamical systems, music cognition, or just want to know more about the details of the approach, read this; Jonah will briefly review/summarize (note: I listed the wrong paper on the syllabus, this is the one I meant).
  • The DAT approach has generated a bunch of literature with specific tests/examinations of the theory in both the music and speech domains. Repp (2010) is a nice example of the musical approach. Rothermich et al. (2012) exemplifies the speech approach. Read these for shorter overviews of the DAT approach and illustrative findings.
  • Because DAT involves 'low-level' audition, it is particularly fruitful for neuro-scientfic approaches. Here are three interesting papers looking at neural correlates of metric attending with some combination of music-like and speech-like stimuli. Claire will summarize/review.
  • Bauer et al. (2015) fail to replicate one of the major results from the early DAT literature. It doesn't look like this is fatal to the whole enterprise or anything, but it seems worth noting.

Week 4 (Oct 15)
Probing stress and foot structure in various and sundry ways:

  • Domahs et al. (2008)) use ERP signatures.
  • Harris & Hyman (2022) use segmental contrasts and positional neutralization.
  • Franich (2021) uses speech cycling.
  • Zuraw & Roca (2025) use musical textsetting.
  • Franich et al. (2025) use co-speech gesture alignment.

Week 5 (Oct 22)
Probing stress and foot structure in even more various and sundry ways:

  • Bennett (2015) uses an Artificial Grammar Learning task to explore the existence of an analytical bias towards feet.
  • Jusczyk & Houston (1999) examine how infants use stress as a segmentation cue at various ages.
  • Johnson & Jusczyk (2001) pit both stress and segmental patterns against statistical regularities and ask how infants resolve the conflict.
  • White et al. (2020) examine asymmetries between final lengthening and initial lengthening in the word-segmentation paradigm across several languages. This is interesting in and of itself, but more narrowly pertinent to this class is their discussion of how stress may or may not condition cross-linguistic differences.

Supplementary papers and miscellaneous links

Things that came up in week 1:

  • Jonah briefly mentioned ambisyllabicity when we were discussing tapping. But what I did on the board was actually not ambisyllabicity proper, because I delinked the intervocalic consonant from the following syllable; this should properly be called resyllabification. In an ambisyllabic analysis, the consonant would remain linked to both syllables. Durvasula and Huang have a 2017 paper that reviews these types of theories (and argues for the resyllabification one).
  • Some of the questions we noted during week 1 involved what makes something 'stress' as opposed to pitch accent or tone or some other category. Hyman's (2006) Phonology paper stakes out one particular position on this, which we don't necessarily endorse, but it is kind of a touchstone for people interested in the question.

Things that came up in week 2:

  • We left out some of the 'heavy-duty' papers that more fully flesh out OT accounts of foot structure. Here is Martinez-Paricio & Kager's (2015) Phonology paper on ternary stress, and Bennett's (2012) UCSC dissertation, which has an OT analysis of the Huariapano patterns we discussed in class (ch. 2).
  • Jonah briefly mentioned some corpus work on the phonetics of 'trapped lights' vs. post-tonic weak syllables in English. Here's a 2025 AMP poster that I haven't managed to turn into a paper yet.
